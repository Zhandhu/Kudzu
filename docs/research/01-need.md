# 01. Why this app should exist

This file makes the case for the purpose of Kudzu: helping graduates of one college stay in touch, and helping students reach them.

The short answer: friendships fade when contact stops, and graduation removes the daily contact that college gave for free. Losing those ties has real costs. People want to reconnect but hold back, mostly because they wrongly expect a cold reply. Alumni networks and general social platforms do not fix this for most graduates. There is fair evidence that direct, one-to-one contact online keeps ties alive. There is no direct evidence that an alumni keep-in-touch app does. That is the main gap, and section 6 says so plainly.

Grades follow the [README](README.md#how-strong-is-the-evidence): A, B, C or H. The "Checked" column says how far each source was read in this round: Full text, Abstract, Summary, or Not re-checked. Full citations are in [sources.md](sources.md).

Most of this research was not done on alumni or on apps. Each section says where the evidence comes from.

## 1. People lose touch after college, and it starts quickly

Friendships need contact to stay close, and they lose closeness within months when contact drops. No study was found that measures exactly how fast college friendships fade after graduation. The evidence below comes from nearby settings.

| Claim | Evidence | Grade | Checked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Friendships lose closeness when contact and shared activities fall. Family ties are less sensitive | Roberts and Dunbar (2011). 25 students followed for 18 months as they left school for university | B | Abstract |
| The number of close contacts stays about the same over seven years, but many of the people change. Losing the chance to meet is an important reason ties end | Mollenhorst, Volker and Flap (2014). Dutch adults, two survey waves | B | Abstract |
| About half of close network members were replaced in seven years | A 2009 press release on the same Dutch survey (1,007 people, 604 re-interviewed). The abstract of the 2014 paper gives no percentage, so this number is not confirmed against the paper | C | Summary |
| People aged about 25 have the most phone contacts. Older age groups have fewer, falling steadily to about 45 | Bhattacharya and others (2016). Call records of 3.2 million users in one European country in 2007 | B | Full text |
| Ties decay fastest when they are new. Ties shared with many mutual contacts last longer. Ties that bridge separate groups decay faster | Burt (2000, 2002). Bankers in one firm over four years | B | Summary |
| Best friends from college lived far apart 19 years later and had moved about six times each. Pairs who had been close for longer in college were more likely to still be close | Ledbetter, Griffin and Sparks (2007). 45 pairs from one small college | B | Summary |
| 47% of US adults lost touch with at least a few friends in the past 12 months | American Perspectives Survey (2021), 2,019 adults. Asked during the pandemic, so the figure may be higher than usual | C | Full text |

What this does and does not tell us:

- The Roberts and Dunbar study is the closest to our case, but it is small and follows the move into university, not out of it (B).
- The phone-records study compares age groups in a single year. It does not follow the same people over time. It shows that 25-year-olds have more contacts than 40-year-olds. It does not prove that each person loses contacts after 25 (B).
- The "about half in seven years" number is widely repeated. We saw it only in press coverage. One blog gives 48%, which we could not confirm (C).
- Making a friend takes many hours together. One study of 112 new students and 355 adults who had moved estimated about 40 to 60 hours to become casual friends and over 200 hours to become close friends (Hall 2018, B, Summary). College provides those hours. Life after college usually does not. We found no figure for how much contact is needed to keep a friendship (H).

## 2. Why the loss matters

Social ties are linked to longer life, to job opportunities and to useful advice. The strongest studies are about relationships in general, not about college friends.

| Claim | Evidence | Grade | Checked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| People with stronger social relationships had 50% higher odds of survival (odds ratio 1.50, 95% CI 1.42 to 1.59) | Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton (2010). Meta-analysis of 148 studies, 308,849 people, average follow-up 7.5 years | A | Full text (abstract and part of the results) |
| Moderately weak ties helped people move jobs more than strong ties did | Rajkumar and others (2022). Randomised experiments on over 20 million LinkedIn members over five years | A | Summary |
| Advice from people you have lost touch with compared well with advice from current contacts | Levin, Walter and Murnighan (2011). 224 executives asked to consult dormant contacts on a work project | A | Summary |
| Friendship across income groups is the social-capital measure most linked to moving up in income | Chetty and others (2022) | C | Not re-checked. Press coverage only |

Limits to keep in mind:

- The survival figure is an odds ratio from observational studies. The authors say that cause is not easily established (A). It does not show that keeping college friends makes anyone live longer.
- The LinkedIn result is about new connections suggested by the platform, and about job moves. It supports the value of weaker ties. It was strongest in more digital industries (A).
- The dormant-ties result comes from executives in MBA classes. It suggests that an old classmate is still worth contacting years later. It was not tested on recent graduates (A for the finding, H for the transfer to our users).

Taken together: old college ties are likely to stay valuable for wellbeing and for careers, even after years of silence. This is a reasonable reading of strong studies from other settings, not a tested fact about alumni.

## 3. Why people do not fix it themselves

People want to reconnect but do not act, because they expect contact to be unwelcome and awkward. This is the best-supported part of the case.

| Claim | Evidence | Grade | Checked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Most people have an old friend they still care about and have lost touch with (90.9% in one study, about 75% in another) | Aknin and Sandstrom (2024). Seven studies, 2,458 people | A | Full text |
| Given time and a ready chance to message an old friend, only 27.8% and 36.8% sent it in two studies | Same paper, studies 3 and 4. Encouragement did not raise the rate | A | Full text |
| The top stated barriers were worry that the friend would not want to hear from them, and that it would feel awkward after so long | Same paper, study 1 | A | Full text |
| People were no more willing to contact an old friend than to talk to a stranger | Same paper, study 5, 288 people | A | Full text |
| First practising on current friends raised the share who messaged an old friend from 31% to 53% | Same paper, study 7, 194 people, randomised | A | Full text |
| People underestimate how much the other person appreciates being contacted. The gap is larger when the contact is a surprise and when the tie is weak | Liu, Rim, Min and Min (2022). A series of experiments, over 5,900 people | A | Summary |
| People underestimate how likely others are to agree to a direct request for help, by as much as 50% | Flynn and Lake (2008) | A | Not re-checked |
| Networking for career gain makes people feel morally "dirty", and those who feel it do it less | Casciaro, Gino and Kouchaki (2014). Three experiments and a survey of lawyers | A | Summary |
| When choosing whom to reconnect with, people prefer those they spent a lot of time with. The most valuable reconnections were with people they had spent less time with, and who were trustworthy and willing to help | Walter, Levin and Murnighan (2015). Executives | B | Summary |

Notes:

- Some press reports of the Aknin and Sandstrom paper say "70% were neutral or negative". The paper reports average scores, not that percentage. We do not use it.
- The Flynn and Lake result was found for requests made in person. A later small study found the opposite over email: people overestimated how well an emailed request to a stranger would work (Roghanizad and Bohns 2017, B, Summary). Requests in an app are text. So the "people will say yes more than you think" claim is weaker for us than it looks. The Liu result is about appreciation of contact, not agreement to a request, and it is the safer one to rely on.
- These findings explain the features. Reminders and a ready reason to write target the "no good reason to reach out" barrier. A request "with a reason" targets the awkwardness of asking. Whether they work in our app is untested (H).

## 4. Why existing channels do not solve it

Few graduates say their alumni network helped them, and the evidence on general social platforms suggests that scrolling and one-click reactions do less than direct messages. The evidence here is weaker than in section 3. It is mostly survey reports.

| Claim | Evidence | Grade | Checked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Only 9% of US graduates said their alumni network was helpful or very helpful in the job market. 22% said unhelpful or very unhelpful. 69% said neither | Strada-Gallup Alumni Survey (2018), 5,107 graduates | C | Full text of the Gallup article |
| At colleges ranked in the top 50, the "helpful" share was 16%. At colleges ranked below 100 it was 8% | Same survey | C | Full text of the Gallup article |
| A median of about 15% of contactable alumni engage with their college in any counted way in a year | CASE Insights on Alumni Engagement (2025), 398 institutions. The figure (14.7% worldwide, 16.3% in the US and Canada) was seen in a search summary and not confirmed against the report | C | Summary |
| About 22% of graduates strongly agree they had a mentor who encouraged them | Gallup-Purdue Index (2014) | C | Not re-checked |
| On Facebook, messages written for one person by close friends were linked to better wellbeing. Broadcast posts and one-click reactions were not | Burke and Kraut (2016). 1,910 users, survey matched to activity logs | B | Summary |

Limits:

- The Strada-Gallup question is about help in the job market. It does not measure whether graduates stay in touch with each other. It is the best number we found, and it measures something next to our question, not our question.
- The CASE figure counts contact with the institution (events, volunteering, giving, reading messages). It does not count contact between classmates. It suggests most alumni are not reached by what colleges do today.
- The Facebook study is not an experiment. People who write to close friends may differ from people who do not (B).
- We found no study showing that general platforms cause college friends to drift apart. The claim is narrower: these platforms make passive contact easy, and the evidence favours direct contact (B).

## 5. What suggests an app can help

Direct online contact between two people is linked to growing closeness, and the effect is larger for people who are rarely in touch. This is support for the mechanism. It is not support for our product.

| Claim | Evidence | Grade | Checked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Communication on Facebook predicted increases in reported closeness, beyond contact through other channels | Burke and Kraut (2014). 3,649 users reporting on 26,134 friends, followed over time | B | Summary |
| The effect was larger for non-family and for infrequent contacts | Same paper, supplement | B | Summary |
| Reading a friend's broadcast posts was also linked to growing closeness | Same paper | B | Summary |
| A short warm-up (messaging current friends first) raised reaching out to old friends by about two-thirds | Aknin and Sandstrom (2024), study 7 | A | Full text |
| Contact with old friends is appreciated more than senders expect | Liu and others (2022) | A | Summary |

Two honest points. First, the Burke and Kraut study is observational, so closeness may drive messaging as much as the reverse (B). Second, it found that passive reading also helped, so we cannot claim that only one-to-one contact works.

## 6. What the evidence does not show

- No independent evaluation of an alumni keep-in-touch app was found. We searched for one and found only vendor case studies, which are in [sources.md](sources.md) at grade C.
- No trial was found showing that reminders to contact friends keep friendships alive. The nearest trials are small apps aimed at loneliness, and neither tested a reminder to contact a friend: both prompted a reflection exercise. One pilot with 29 students found no significant change in loneliness or connectedness, and users found the prompts badly timed (B, Summary). One four-week trial with 99 older adults is reported as finding no effect on loneliness, but that result was not confirmed against the paper (B, Summary). [02-choices.md](02-choices.md) describes both in more detail.
- No study was found that measures how fast college friendships fade after graduation, or how much contact keeps one alive. Our "closeness fades without contact" display is built on the Roberts and Dunbar finding and on judgement. The fade rate is our own guess (H).
- Showing a person that a friendship is "fading" could motivate them or could make them feel guilty and avoid the app. We found no evidence either way (H).
- The wellbeing evidence is about relationships in general and is not causal. We cannot promise users that the app improves health or happiness.
- The career evidence is about weak and dormant ties among executives and LinkedIn members. It was not measured for students contacting alumni.
- Almost all studies are from the United States and Western Europe. Results may differ elsewhere.
- Several numbers above were seen only in summaries. They are marked in the tables.

## So what should the app do

Each requirement follows from the evidence above. Each still needs testing with our own users.

1. Make direct, one-to-one contact the main action. Feeds and reactions should not be the centre. (Burke and Kraut 2014 and 2016, B)
2. Tell people that their message will be welcome, at the moment they hesitate. The belief that it will not be is the main barrier, and it is wrong on average. (Aknin and Sandstrom 2024, A; Liu and others 2022, A)
3. Give people a reason and an easy first step. Offer an occasion such as a birthday, a class announcement or a shared event, and let people start with friends they already talk to. (Aknin and Sandstrom 2024, A)
4. Treat a quiet friendship as worth reviving, not as lost. Suggest people the user would not think of first, not only the closest old friends. (Levin and others 2011, A; Walter and others 2015, B)
5. Keep asking for help separate from, and lighter than, "networking". One request, to one person, with a stated reason. Do not promise a high rate of yes for text requests. (Casciaro and others 2014, A; Roghanizad and Bohns 2017, B)
6. Measure whether it works. Record contact kept and contact revived, and compare users who get reminders with users who do not. No one has published this for an alumni app, so our own data will be the first evidence. (H)
